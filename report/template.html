Re-analysis of Greenberg, Fisher & Walker, SNAPL 2015 · data: pldb

Tracking the flow of ideas through the PL literature, ten years on

The 2015 paper fit a 20-topic LDA model to 4,355 abstracts from ICFP, OOPSLA, PLDI and POPL and read trends off loess curves. This redo uses the pldb corpus ( abstracts from 24 venues, 1973–2026), structural topic models with venue × time covariates and real uncertainty, citation-trained document embeddings, and a citation graph for validating "related work" claims at scale.

    §2 · Data and model choice

    The corpus, and how many topics to fit

    pldb holds papers from dblp. have abstracts. Coverage is nearly complete for the four big conferences and patchy for Springer venues (CAV, TACAS, SAS, VMCAI, ESOP, ECOOP, APLAS), so the 24-venue analyses weight those venues by what is available. Each document is title + abstract. Author names were left out, unlike the original, because they let a model match on people instead of ideas.

    Documents per year in the analysis corpus. The four conferences studied in 2015 are shown separately; "Other 20 venues" is used only in the all-venue analyses (§9 and §10).

    The 2015 paper fixed k = 20 and said that no method existed for choosing k. For the structural topic model, held-out log-likelihood improves steadily until about K = 50 and then flattens. Semantic coherence falls as K grows and exclusivity plateaus near 40. K = 40 sits at the knee. The 20-topic LDA replication scores well on NPMI coherence, but its topics mix unrelated themes (see the replication list at the end).

    Compiler optimization: down, as the paper saw, and it kept falling

    The paper reported "strong downward trends through the 2000s" for optimization. The decline continued. The Code optimization & codegen topic (register allocation, inlining, specialization, instruction selection) fell at PLDI from 17.6% of abstracts [95% CI 15.7–19.3] in 1995 to 7.7% in 2008 and 2.8% [1.6–4.0] in 2025. Compilers & verified compilation (LLVM, CompCert, Wasm, translation validation) stayed flat at about 3.4%. The change is from making code fast to making compilers trustworthy.

    §5 · The OOPSLA question

    Did the 2007 call for papers and the 2010 SPLASH rebrand change OOPSLA?

    "Did generalizing the Call for Papers for OOPSLA in 2007 or changing the name of the umbrella conference to SPLASH in 2010 have any effect on the kinds of papers published there?" (SNAPL 2015)

    The 2015 paper answered this visually: OO programming declined "beginning in around the year 2000… with little apparent dip" at the rebrand. The model-based answer is that both are true. OO content at OOPSLA was already falling before 2007. After netting out that pre-trend and field-wide year effects, there is still an additional step down at both 2007 and 2010.

    Share of each paper devoted to the two OO topics (Objects, inheritance & GC + OO design & environments): OOPSLA minus the other three conferences, per year, with 95% bootstrap CI. Dashed lines mark 2007 (new CFP) and 2010 (SPLASH).

    Paper-level OLS on the OO share, 1997–2019, standard errors clustered by venue-year. Coefficients are in percentage points of an abstract's topic mass.

    OOPSLA did broaden. Its effective number of topics (the exponential of the Shannon entropy of its mean topic mixture) rose from about 13 in 1990 to 33 in 2020, overtaking POPL, which has narrowed since 2010. But the rise is a smooth 30-year curve. There is no visible kink at 2007 or 2010, and a PELT changepoint search on OOPSLA's yearly topic shares puts the break for the OO-runtime topic at 2005, before the CFP change. The topics that took OO's place at OOPSLA are Testing & bug finding (0.5% in 1995 to 9.0% in 2025), Synthesis & learning (0.3% to 7.4%) and Verification & separation logic (0.3% to 5.9%). In 2015 the paper saw only the start of this, as "test generation, analysis of concurrent programming, parallelism, and verification."

    §6 · Convergence

    The four conferences are becoming one conference

    The 2015 paper started from "the prevailing wisdom… that each of the four major SIGPLAN conferences has its own character." That is measurably less true every decade. Jensen–Shannon divergence between venues' topic mixtures has fallen for every pair. OOPSLA and PLDI are now nearly indistinguishable (JSD 0.35 in 1990 → 0.03 in 2025). A logistic classifier on SPECTER2 embeddings, cross-fitted over ten folds, identified a paper's venue from its abstract 69% of the time in the early 1990s but only 43% of the time since 2025, against a 25% chance rate.

    §7 · PLDI vs. POPL

    Where POPL and PLDI differ, then and now

    The paper found PLDI "relatively stable" on types while POPL's weight "steadily increased", a POPL spike in continuations and partial evaluation in the 1990s, and PLDI persistently ahead on parallelization and dynamic analysis. All three reproduce. In the chart, each topic's POPL:PLDI ratio (log2) is shown for 1988–2004 and 2012–2026. Almost every dot moved toward zero: Type theory & dependent types went from 40× more POPL to 9×, and Formal semantics from 4.8× to 1.5×. Code optimization went from 5.4× more PLDI to 3.6×.

    §8 · Separation logic at POPL

    Did O'Hearn's separation logic shift POPL?

    "Have the ideas in O'Hearn's classic paper on separation logic shifted the kinds of papers that appear in POPL?" (SNAPL 2015)

    The 2015 paper asked this question and left it for future work. Three independent signals give the same answer. A segmented logistic model of the share of POPL papers citing the founding papers (Ishtiaq–O'Hearn POPL'01, O'Hearn–Reynolds–Yang CSL'01, Reynolds LICS'02, O'Hearn CONCUR'04) puts the break at 2004–2005 (posterior mass 0.91 under a flat prior). Within two years, about one POPL paper in five cited them. Direct citation of the founding papers then fades as the field cites its own descendants (Iris, RustBelt, concurrent separation logics). The topic signal keeps growing, though: the Verification & separation logic topic rose from 1.2% of POPL in 1995 to 7.3% [5.9–8.9] in 2025, and it spread to PLDI, ICFP and OOPSLA at the same scale. The POPL papers that cite separation logic are much less about model checking and dependent type theory, and much more about concurrency and layered abstraction.

    §9 · Finding your friends

    Are cited papers closer than random ones, and which model finds them best?

    The 2015 paper tested four papers (CDRS, PCC, TAL, SEMC) and found that citations usually sat closer in LDA space than random papers did. With OpenAlex reference lists, the test runs on every paper that cites at least five earlier papers in the corpus. The candidate pool for each query is every earlier paper. The paper's own finding holds: under its exact method (k = 20, Euclidean distance), a cited paper outranks a random uncited one 78% of the time. But that representation is a weak recommender, with only 8.6% of references in the top 20. A 40-topic STM more than doubles that. Plain TF-IDF and SPECTER2 embeddings roughly triple it. TF-IDF is best at putting a true reference first (highest MRR), and SPECTER2 has the best overall ranking (AUC). SPECTER2 was trained on Semantic Scholar citation links, which may include these papers, so its numbers are optimistic. TF-IDF has no such leakage.

    AUC: probability a cited paper ranks above a random uncited one (ICFP/OOPSLA/PLDI/POPL, 3,940 queries, 95% bootstrap CI).

    Recall@20: fraction of a paper's in-corpus references in its top 20 suggestions. The 2015 tool returned 20.

    The four case studies, and the Nix anecdote

    For the paper's four examples, the table shows the median percentile rank (lower = closer) of each paper's in-corpus ICFP/OOPSLA/PLDI/POPL citations versus random papers. PCC (POPL'97) cites no earlier papers from these four venues in OpenAlex, which fits the original finding that its related work was hard to place. The paper's best anecdote was that the tool surfaced Nix's Editing by Example (POPL'84) for Osera & Zdancewic's PLDI'15 synthesis paper. Rerun here, that recovery is fragile. Among 4,024 earlier papers, Nix ranks 2,907th under the 20-topic Euclidean model, 173rd under STM, 138th under TF-IDF and 1,315th under SPECTER2.

    §10 · Beyond the big four

    Twenty-four venues through an embedding-based topic model

    The paper's first stated limitation was "our corpora include only data from four programming language conferences." BERTopic clusters SPECTER2 embeddings of all abstracts (UMAP → HDBSCAN → class-based TF-IDF). It finds sharply defined topics, such as gradual typing, Iris/concurrent separation logic, smart contracts and SV-COMP tool papers, that a 20-topic LDA cannot resolve. HDBSCAN initially left 55% of documents unclustered; each of those was assigned to its nearest topic centroid in embedding space. The heatmap shows each venue's fingerprint.

    §11 · Robustness and limits

    How much should you trust any one topic model?

    The 2015 paper compared abstract- and full-text-based models. pldb has no full text, so the analogue here compares model families on the same abstracts. The normalized mutual information between each model's hard topic assignments is only moderate: . The individual topics depend on the model. The headline trends above (OO decline, optimization decline, convergence, the rise of separation logic, testing, synthesis and probabilistic programming) appear in STM, in BERTopic and in raw text or citation counts, which is why they are reported as findings.

    Limits: abstracts only; Springer-venue coverage is partial; OpenAlex reference lists are incomplete for older ACM papers; topic names are human judgments; the DiD design assumes the other three conferences are a valid counterfactual for OOPSLA. That assumption is plausible for field-wide shocks but untestable for OOPSLA-specific ones, so the pre-trend-adjusted estimate is the one to quote.
